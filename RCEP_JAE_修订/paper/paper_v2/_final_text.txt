   RCEP and Supply Chain Relationships of Chinese
    Firms: Evidence from Relationship-Pair Data
                          Anonymous manuscript




Abstract
We examine how the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership
(RCEP) is associated with the international supply-chain relationships
of Chinese listed firms. Using FactSet Revere disclosures, we construct
a balanced panel of 630,280 relationship–year observations for 78,785
directed customer and supplier pairs involving 5,689 firms over 2017–2024.
A difference-in-differences specification with firm–partner–direction pair
fixed effects estimates a 1.36 percentage-point increase in the probability
that an RCEP relationship is active at year-end relative to relationships
with other foreign partners. The estimate is similar under member-specific
implementation dates and several alternative samples, although it is
sensitive to a restrictive Asian comparison group. Partner-country–year
evidence is consistent with three policy-linked pathways: tariff relief, a
larger intermediate-goods share in China’s imports from member economies,
and a lower partner-country import share. The response is concentrated in
supplier relationships, for which the estimated differential increase is 2.95
percentage points; the estimate for customer relationships is statistically
indistinguishable from zero. The joint Wald test does not reject equality
of the four pre-treatment coefficients to zero at the 5% level (p = 0.078),
but does reject at the 10% level. The results point to an economically
meaningful relationship-level response while also indicating the limits of
identification over a short post-entry period.
Keywords: RCEP, supply chains, relationship-pair panel,
difference-in-differences, trade policy
1. Introduction
    Production in Asia is organized through dense cross-border networks in
which firms repeatedly source inputs from, and sell output to, partners in
neighboring economies. Recent evidence points to considerable persistence
in the region’s aggregate trade network even after a large common shock
(Kiyota, 2022), while new measures of regional value-chain position empha-
size the importance of tracing production links beyond gross trade flows
(He et al., 2023). The entry into force of the Regional Comprehensive Eco-
nomic Partnership (RCEP) on January 1, 2022 began bringing 15 Asia-Pacific
economies under a common trade framework. Its combination of coordinated
tariff schedules, regional cumulation of origin, and trade-facilitation commit-
ments can change both the cost of a cross-border transaction and the set of
partners with which that transaction is feasible. Whether this institutional
integration changes relationships between firms, rather than only aggregate
trade, remains an open empirical question.
    This paper studies that question using relationship-level data. We convert
disclosed customer and supplier records in the FactSet Revere database into
a balanced annual panel of 630,280 observations for 78,785 directed firm–
partner pairs. The empirical design compares relationships with partners in
RCEP member economies to relationships with other foreign partners before
and after the common 2022 treatment date. The specification includes firm–
partner–direction pair fixed effects and year fixed effects, with standard errors
clustered by relationship pair.
    The main estimate is 0.0136 (standard error 0.0040), implying a 1.36
percentage-point differential increase in the probability that a relationship
is active at year-end. The estimate is 0.0144 (standard error 0.0041) when
member-specific entry dates are used and remains positive in several alter-
native time windows and sample exclusions. Its sign reverses, however, when
the comparison group is restricted to seven Asian non-members, showing
that the estimated contrast depends on the choice of non-RCEP counterfac-
tual. The response is concentrated in supplier relationships, for which the
estimated increase is 2.95 percentage points; the corresponding estimate for
customer relationships is small and statistically insignificant. At the partner-
country–year level, RCEP exposure is associated with tariff relief, a higher
intermediate-goods share in China’s imports from member economies, and
a lower partner-country share of China’s imports. These aggregate trade
patterns, together with the supplier-side relationship response, are consis-

                                       2
tent with stronger regional production-network integration helping Chinese
firms maintain or establish disclosed supplier relationships. The joint pre-
treatment Wald test does not reject at the 5% level but does reject at the
10% level (p = 0.078). We therefore interpret the estimates as a positive
relative response while keeping the limitations of both the timing evidence
and the comparison group explicit.
    The paper is positioned against several closely related bodies of work.
Research on trade agreements has established effects on bilateral trade, firm
outcomes, and third-country adjustment (Baier and Bergstrand, 2007; Sun
et al., 2022; Lee et al., 2023), while recent RCEP studies evaluate regional
value chains in a GVC-CGE framework (Sun et al., 2023) or examine how fi-
nancing constraints relate to manufacturers’ export partnerships with RCEP
economies (Peng et al., 2025). A second literature studies firms’ use of prefer-
ences and the sourcing consequences of rules of origin (Hayakawa et al., 2023;
Tanaka and Fukunishi, 2022; Head et al., 2024). A third analyzes how trade-
policy changes propagate through supplier search and firm-to-firm production
links (Garg et al., 2023; Grossman et al., 2024). These studies motivate a
relationship-level test but do not establish whether RCEP’s entry into force
altered the annual activity of directed customer and supplier links.
    Relative to this work, the paper makes three contributions. First, it shifts
the outcome from aggregate trade or export participation to the annual activ-
ity of disclosed firm–partner relationships and estimates the policy response
within the same directed pair over time. Second, it relates partner-country–
year changes in tariff relief, the composition of intermediate-goods imports,
and partner-country import shares to relationship activity, while recognizing
that these aggregate measures do not observe firm-level preference claims,
certificates of origin, customs transactions, or relationship values. Third, it
separates supplier from customer links and examines how the initial rela-
tionship response varies with pre-policy relationship maturity, prior trade-
agreement coverage, and manufacturing exposure.
    The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews
the literature and develops the hypotheses. Section 3 describes the sample,
variables, and empirical design. Section 4 presents the baseline estimates,
mechanism analysis, and robustness tests. Section 5 examines heterogeneous
responses. Section 6 provides further evidence on a competing explanation.
Section 7 concludes with policy implications.

2. Literature review and hypothesis development
2.1. Literature review
    The first strand of related research examines how trade agreements and
trade institutions affect bilateral trade and firm behavior. Gravity-based evi-
dence establishes that free-trade agreements increase trade between members
(Baier and Bergstrand, 2007). Recent JAE studies extend the analysis from
trade flows to export-product quality and foreign direct investment, examine
environmental provisions as a determinant of firm-level quality adjustment,
and estimate employment responses to regional integration (Sun et al., 2022;
Narayan et al., 2022; Zhu and Sun, 2025; Li et al., 2025). The incidence of an
agreement nevertheless depends on the institutional details faced by firms.
Hayakawa et al. (2023), for example, measure the firm-level utilization of
regional trade agreements from the importer’s perspective, while Ni et al.
(2022) analyze heterogeneous trade effects across sectors. Other recent work
examines how tariff costs and trade-policy uncertainty shape cross-border
organization, export behavior, and input choice (Xie, 2023; Zhou and Wen,
2022; Handley et al., 2024; Poilly and Tripier, 2025). Firm-level evidence
on third-country effects further shows why adjustment outside an agreement
must be part of the comparison (Lee et al., 2023). This literature implies that
membership and statutory tariff schedules alone are insufficient measures of
firms’ effective exposure.
    The second strand studies regional production networks and RCEP more
directly. Recent work constructs measures of regional dual value chains (He
et al., 2023), evaluates RCEP through a GVC-CGE model (Sun et al., 2023),
and examines the reorganization of export-product structures as global value
chains change (Zhang and Ding, 2024). Complementary JAE studies exam-
ine domestic value added in exports, GVC upgrading after foreign-capital
withdrawal, financial and institutional determinants of value-chain partici-
pation, and risk-related changes in regional trade structure (Du et al., 2023;
Wu et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Ali et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). At
the firm level, Peng et al. (2025) study financing constraints and export
partnerships with RCEP economies. Other evidence connects lower cross-
border mobility costs to bilateral value-chain linkages (Ma et al., 2024b) and
digital development to economies’ positions in the intermediate-goods trade
network (Huang et al., 2026). Work on data restrictions and digital-trade
provisions further illustrates that contemporary regional integration operates
through non-tariff rules as well as customs duties (Ma et al., 2024a; Li and Li,

                                       4
2026). These contributions establish the relevance of regional integration for
production networks, but aggregate value-chain measures, simulations, and
exporter-country links do not reveal whether a particular disclosed customer
or supplier relationship remains active after implementation.
     The third strand concerns rules of origin and the organization of input
sourcing. Rules of origin determine whether firms can claim preferences
and can therefore either promote regional sourcing or impose compliance
costs. Tanaka and Fukunishi (2022) find that relaxing origin requirements
produced heterogeneous export responses across developing-economy ben-
eficiaries. Studying reforms in Japan, Hayakawa and Yamanouchi (2023)
document an increase in imports from least-developed economies but hetero-
geneous effects on domestic producers. Theoretically, Head et al. (2024) show
that tighter content requirements initially expand regional parts sourcing but
reduce it once the requirement passes a threshold. The design of these rules
is itself endogenous to negotiation and differs between intermediate and final
products (Hayakawa et al., 2024); overlapping preference regimes add another
layer to firms’ compliance choices (Hayakawa, 2022). Recent JAE evidence
also links rules of origin to imported inputs and firm innovation (Zhou and
Wu, 2025). Taken together, this work makes input sourcing central to the
expected effect of RCEP’s regional-cumulation provision.
     A final set of studies highlights that supply chains are networks of durable
relationships rather than anonymous spot transactions. Administrative ship-
ment data reveal that ownership boundaries, distance, relationship speci-
ficity, and input characteristics shape where firms source products (Garg et
al., 2023). Models of unexpected tariffs similarly predict renegotiation with
incumbent suppliers or costly search for replacement partners (Grossman et
al., 2024). Empirical studies connect imported inputs and supply-chain links
to firm productivity (Newman et al., 2023), show that shocks can propa-
gate through observed firm networks (Crosignani et al., 2022), and examine
supply-chain spillovers in corporate innovation (Ou et al., 2026). The present
paper joins this relationship-based perspective to the RCEP literature. Its
empirical unit preserves the firm, partner, and relationship direction, allow-
ing the analysis to distinguish changes in relationship activity from changes
in trade values or aggregate network position.

2.2. Hypothesis development
    RCEP was signed on November 15, 2020 and entered into force for the first
group of members on January 1, 2022. The agreement covers China, Japan,

                                       5
South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, and the ten ASEAN economies. It
combines market-access commitments with rules on origin, customs proce-
dures, services, investment, intellectual property, and electronic commerce
(Shen and Li, 2020; Ping, 2021). Recent research on digital-trade rules in
regional agreements underscores the network implications of this broader
institutional scope (Li and Li, 2026). RCEP’s common origin regime is espe-
cially relevant in Asia, where firms previously navigated several overlapping
ASEAN-plus-one agreements whose product-level rules reflected different ne-
gotiating interests (Hayakawa, 2022; Hayakawa et al., 2024).
    The tariff schedule is phased. Approximately 65 percent of tariff lines re-
ceive immediate elimination, while other lines are reduced over five to twenty
years and a small set is excluded. More than 90 percent of tariff lines are
scheduled eventually to reach zero, but the timing differs by product and
member (Liu and Zhen, 2022). The 2022–2024 observation window therefore
captures the initial institutional response and only the early portion of tariff
liberalization; it should not be read as an estimate of the agreement’s full
long-run tariff effect.
    The value of a supply-chain relationship reflects more than the current
tariff on a shipment. Establishing a link requires partner search, screening,
negotiation, and adaptation to relationship-specific production needs. Once
formed, the link remains active when the expected surplus from continued
trade exceeds the cost of renegotiation or replacement. This logic is consistent
with evidence that relationship specificity and input characteristics shape
firms’ sourcing choices (Garg et al., 2023) and with models in which tariff
shocks induce renegotiation with incumbent suppliers or costly search for
alternatives (Grossman et al., 2024).
    RCEP raises the expected surplus from qualifying intraregional trade
through phased tariff reductions, common procedures, and regional cumula-
tion. Value added from any member can count toward the originating status
of a product, subject to the agreement’s regional-value-content rules. The
provision broadens the set of production arrangements capable of receiving
preferential treatment and reduces the need to satisfy separate origin systems
for each bilateral route. Utilization is not automatic—firms must still find
compliance worthwhile (Hayakawa et al., 2023)—but the joint reduction in
trade and coordination costs can both sustain an existing link and make a
newly disclosed link worthwhile. This reasoning yields the first hypothesis:
H1. RCEP’s entry into force increases the probability that a disclosed supply-
chain relationship between a Chinese listed firm and a partner in an RCEP

                                       6
economy is active.
     The agreement can sustain relationships through three related channels.
First, incremental tariff relief raises the net return to transactions with part-
ners for which RCEP improves on the pre-existing tariff schedule. This mar-
gin should be strongest where the additional preference is economically mean-
ingful, but its effect depends on actual utilization rather than the statutory
rate alone. Second, regional cumulation increases the value of intermediate
inputs obtained within the agreement because content contributed by one
member can help a downstream product qualify in another. Evidence that
origin rules affect export responses (Tanaka and Fukunishi, 2022; Hayakawa
and Yamanouchi, 2023) and theoretical results linking content requirements
to regional parts sourcing (Head et al., 2024) support this input-reallocation
channel. Recent firm-level work that connects origin rules with imported
inputs provides a closely related motivation (Zhou and Wu, 2025).
     Third, a common origin regime can change the concentration of eligible
sourcing. Under separate bilateral agreements, a firm may organize produc-
tion around a narrow set of partners that satisfy route-specific requirements.
Cumulation makes inputs from multiple member economies mutually usable
for origin purposes, widening the feasible regional supplier set and reducing
dependence on any one partner source. Research on import diversification
under a regional cooperation initiative provides a related application of this
sourcing margin (Zhu et al., 2025). This prediction does not require every
firm to diversify immediately: origin rules may generate nonlinear responses
(Head et al., 2024), and reforms have produced heterogeneous effects across
products and firms (Tanaka and Fukunishi, 2022; Hayakawa and Yamanouchi,
2023). It instead implies that, on average, tariff relief, stronger intermediate-
goods trade integration among members, and a lower partner-country import
share are observable implications of the proposed channels. These trade mea-
sures are evaluated at the partner-country–year level. Their relationship-level
implication is that deeper regional production integration can make supplier
ties more valuable to maintain or establish. These arguments yield the second
hypothesis:
H2. The relationship response to RCEP is associated with incremental tar-
iff relief, stronger intermediate-goods trade integration among members, and
reduced dependence on any one partner-country source.
     The relevance of these channels should differ by relationship direction. A
supplier relationship records a foreign partner providing inputs or products
to the Chinese firm, whereas a customer relationship records sales by the Chi-

                                       7
nese firm to a foreign partner. Tariff preferences can affect both directions,
but regional cumulation operates first through the origin, eligibility, and or-
ganization of inputs. The supplier side is therefore exposed directly to the
decision margins emphasized by research on imported-input choice (Handley
et al., 2024), within-firm and external sourcing (Garg et al., 2023), and the
productivity role of imports transmitted through supply chains (Newman et
al., 2023). Customer relationships may respond only after changes in input
composition, production costs, or market access pass through to downstream
sales. Over the first three years of implementation, this ordering implies a
larger and more readily detectable response among supplier links. We there-
fore propose the third hypothesis:
H3. The increase in relationship activity following RCEP is stronger for
supplier relationships than for customer relationships.

3. Research design
3.1. Sample and data
    The relationship data are from FactSet Revere’s Supply Chain Relation-
ships database. We retain CUSTOMER and SUPPLIER records involving a
Chinese listed firm and a foreign partner, and preserve the direction of the
link. The cleaned source contains 578,142 eligible disclosure records for 2017–
2024. Multiple disclosure episodes involving the same Chinese firm, partner,
and direction are consolidated into 78,785 distinct relationship pairs. Ex-
panding each pair over the eight calendar years yields the balanced panel of
630,280 relationship–year observations used in the baseline analysis.

                                       8
    Firm financial information is from the China Stock Market and Account-
ing Research (CSMAR) database. We retain annual consolidated statements,
exclude financial firms and ST firms, and winsorize continuous firm variables
at the first and 99th percentiles. The matched firm-year file contains 35,978
observations for 5,689 firms (including 3,643 manufacturing firms).
    Tariff data combine most-favored-nation rates with official additional-
duty schedules from a major non-RCEP import market. Product-level rates
are mapped from HS8 products to two-digit national industry categories
using a 5,724-row concordance. The industry exposure series uses fixed 2016
Chinese export weights. For the further analysis, firm-level exposure is fixed
in 2019 and equals the export-weighted average of the applicable additional
duties. Neither measure uses post-policy trade shares.

3.2. Variable selection and description
3.2.1. Supply-chain relationship activity
    The unit of observation is a Chinese firm–foreign partner–direction pair
observed at the end of each calendar year. Let i index the Chinese firm, j the
foreign partner, and r the direction (supplier or customer). The outcome,
Activeijrt , equals one if at least one disclosure episode for pair (i, j, r) has
started by December 31 of year t and has not ended by that date; it is
zero otherwise. Thus, the panel records the extensive margin of relationship
activity among pairs observed at least once in the source data, rather than
the probability of forming every possible firm combination.

3.2.2. RCEP treatment
    The treatment is P ost2022t × RCEPj . In the common-date specifica-
tion, P ost2022t equals one for calendar years 2022–2024 and zero for 2017–
2021. RCEPj equals one when the partner is located in one of the 14 RCEP
economies other than China. This specification treats 2022, when the agree-
ment first entered into force, as the common policy date. A sensitivity spec-
ification instead assigns 2023 to Indonesia and the Philippines, whose imple-
mentation dates were later. The relationship-pair fixed effect, αijr , absorbs
all time-invariant characteristics of a disclosed pair, while year fixed effects
absorb common annual shocks.

3.2.3. Covariates and auxiliary measures
    Table 1 reports the variables used in the analysis. The firm covariates
are the log of total assets (“Size”), book leverage (“Lev”), return on assets

                                       9
(“ROA”), annual revenue growth (“Growth”), and inventory divided by to-
tal assets (“InvDensity”). They are defined at the firm-year level; the pre-
ferred fixed-effects estimate is intentionally identified from the treatment
interaction and pair and year fixed effects, while these covariates enter the
high-dimensional nuisance models in the double-machine-learning sensitiv-
ity analysis. The pre-policy relationship-age measure is the number of years
from the first disclosed start date to December 31, 2021 for pairs active in
2021. Pre-policy sourcing breadth counts the distinct RCEP partner coun-
tries connected to a Chinese firm through supplier links in 2021; the large-
firm indicator is based on the median of firms’ 2017–2021 mean log assets.
Manufacturing status is based on the firm’s latest available primary-industry
classification observed no later than 2021.
    The policy-linked indicators are defined at the partner-country–year level
and merged to every relationship involving that country. Incremental tariff
relief is the 2017–2019 import-weighted scheduled tariff reduction under the
relevant RCEP schedule. The intermediate-goods share is China’s imports
of goods classified as intermediate inputs under the strict BEC5 definition
from partner country j, divided by China’s total imports from that partner
in year t. It measures the composition of bilateral imports and, across RCEP
members, the degree of regional intermediate-goods trade integration; it is
not a measure of firm i’s input purchases. The partner-country import share
is country j’s share of China’s total imports in year t; a lower value indicates
less dependence on that individual source. These aggregates describe policy-
relevant conditions and do not measure a firm’s preference claim, certificate
of origin, transaction value, or overall firm-level sourcing concentration.

3.3. Model specification
    The empirical design exploits the common entry-into-force date while al-
lowing exposure to vary across partner economies. We compare the evolution
of relationships involving RCEP partners with that of relationships involv-
ing other foreign partners. Pair fixed effects remove time-invariant differences
across disclosed firm–partner–direction pairs, and year fixed effects remove
shocks common to all relationships. Identification therefore comes from the
differential post-2022 change between the two partner groups. The identi-
fying condition is that, absent RCEP, their relationship activity would have
followed parallel trends. It also requires the comparison group not to expe-
rience differential spillovers from the agreement. That requirement may be
imperfect because RCEP can redirect sourcing and sales between member

                                      10
and non-member economies: trade diversion that reduces non-member rela-
tionships would enlarge the estimated contrast, whereas positive spillovers to
non-members would attenuate it. The coefficient should therefore be inter-
preted as the post-entry change for RCEP relationships relative to non-RCEP
relationships, rather than as an isolated effect against a completely untreated
counterfactual. We estimate a linear probability model because the outcome
is an indicator and interpret the coefficient as a percentage-point change.
    The baseline specification is

               Activeijrt = β(P ost2022t × RCEPj ) + αijr + λt + εijrt ,                                         (1)

where αijr denotes firm–partner–direction pair fixed effects and λt denotes
year fixed effects. The coefficient β is the differential change in the probability
that an observed relationship is active for RCEP partners relative to other
foreign partners. Standard errors are clustered by relationship pair, matching
the level at which serially correlated relationship outcomes and treatment
histories are observed.

4.2. Mechanism analysis
    The theoretical discussion identifies three policy-linked channels as-
sociated with relationship activity: incremental tariff relief, regional
intermediate-goods trade integration, and reduced dependence on an
individual partner-country source. Each measure varies at the partner-
country–year level and is assigned to relationship (i, j, r) according to the
partner’s country. Denoting the relevant country-level measure by Mjt , we
estimate


            Mjt = a(P ost2022t × RCEPj ) + αijr + λt + uijrt ,                     (2)
       Activeijrt = c′ (P ost2022t × RCEPj ) + bMjt + αijr + λt + vijrt .          (3)

    Equations (2) and (3) define the two paths. For each indicator, the total
effect c is re-estimated from Eq. (1) on the same complete-case sample used
in the two path equations; this is why the reported values of c differ slightly

                                          13
across rows and from the full-sample estimate in Table 2. The indirect as-
sociation is ab, and the linear decomposition satisfies c = c′ + ab. Inference
for ab is based on 2,000 bootstrap samples drawn at the relationship-pair
level; both path equations are re-estimated in every draw. This procedure
preserves the panel structure and uses the same fixed effects and clustering
level as the corresponding regressions.
    Table 3 reports the estimates. The percentile 95% bootstrap confidence
intervals exclude zero for all three indirect associations: [0.0087, 0.0209] for
incremental tariff relief, [0.0002, 0.0004] for the intermediate-goods share of
bilateral imports, and [0.0240, 0.0307] for the partner-country import share.
RCEP exposure is associated with a 0.0176 increase in the intermediate-
goods share, equivalent to 1.76 percentage points. The corresponding indi-
rect association with relationship activity is 0.0003, or 0.03 percentage points.
This estimate indicates stronger intermediate-goods trade integration among
RCEP members, not increased input purchases by any particular firm. Com-
bined with the supplier-side estimate reported below, the result is consistent
with regional production-network integration helping Chinese firms maintain
or establish disclosed supplier relationships. RCEP exposure is also associ-
ated with a lower partner-country import share, and a lower share is in turn
associated with more active relationships. In this third decomposition, how-
ever, the positive indirect association (ab = 0.0274) is offset by a negative
direct coefficient (c′ = −0.0141); it should not be interpreted as a propor-
tion of the total effect. Overall, the three patterns are consistent with H2 as
policy-linked channel evidence, but do not identify firm-level preference use,
certification, or transaction-level adjustment.

4.3. Robustness tests
4.3.1. Event-study test
    Figure 3 plots the event-study coefficients relative to the year before entry

four degrees of freedom (p = 0.078). The null is therefore not rejected at
the 5% level but is rejected at the 10% level. Accordingly, the event study
provides only marginal support for the parallel-trends assumption rather than
a definitive validation of it.

to the composition of the non-RCEP comparison group, a concern related

7. Conclusion and policy recommendations
     This paper examines how RCEP’s entry into force relates to the inter-
national supply-chain relationships of Chinese listed firms. In a panel of
630,280 relationship–year observations and 78,785 relationship pairs, the pre-
ferred specification yields a 0.0136 differential increase in the probability that
an RCEP relationship is active at year-end. The corresponding estimate is
0.0144 under member-specific implementation dates. The response is concen-
trated in supplier relationships, whose estimated increase is 0.0295, compared
with an insignificant estimate of -0.0033 for customer relationships. It is also
larger for Japan than for members with prior free-trade-agreement coverage
(a difference of 0.0403) and, among supplier links, for manufacturing than for
non-manufacturing firms (a difference of 0.0479). At the partner-country–
year level, the bootstrap indirect associations are 0.0150 for incremental tar-
iff relief (95% CI [0.0087, 0.0209]), 0.0003 for the intermediate-goods share
of bilateral imports (95% CI [0.0002, 0.0004]), and 0.0274 through a lower
partner-country import share (95% CI [0.0240, 0.0307]). These aggregate
patterns, together with the supplier-side response, are consistent with RCEP
strengthening regional production-network integration and thereby support-
ing the maintenance or formation of disclosed supplier relationships.
     The findings carry three policy implications. First, implementation ef-
forts should give firms clear and accessible guidance on regional cumulation,
tariff schedules, and documentary requirements. The value of a common
agreement depends not only on formal concessions but also on whether firms
can apply the rules at reasonable administrative cost. Second, the increase in
the member-level intermediate-goods share, together with the supplier-side
relationship response, suggests that trade-facilitation policies should support
the matching of firms with qualified regional suppliers. Third, policymakers
should monitor whether the early expansion of regional links reduces depen-
dence on individual sources while preserving resilience to disruptions. These
measures would help translate formal integration into durable firm-level re-
lationships.
     The evidence should nevertheless be interpreted within the limits of the
design. The joint Wald test of the four pre-treatment event-study coefficients
does not reject at the 5% level but is marginal at the 10% level (χ2 (4) = 8.389,
p = 0.078). The non-RCEP comparison group may itself respond to trade
diversion or production-network spillovers generated by RCEP and is there-
fore not guaranteed to provide a completely untreated counterfactual. The

                                       20
estimate remains positive after excluding the largest non-RCEP comparison
market and offshore financial centers, but its sign reverses when the com-
parison group is restricted to seven Asian non-members. This sensitivity
reinforces the interpretation of the baseline coefficient as a relative change
between partner groups rather than the agreement’s total effect. FactSet Re-
vere primarily covers listed and large firms and does not report transaction
values, customs declarations, certificates of origin, or firms’ actual preference
utilization. Moreover, the 2022–2024 period captures only the initial phase of
a tariff schedule extending over several years. Linking product-level customs
records to firm identifiers and extending the post-entry window would allow
future research to distinguish relationship formation, survival, and trade-
value responses more precisely.
